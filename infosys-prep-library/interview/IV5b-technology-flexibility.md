# IV5 — Are you willing to work in any technology?

*Adapted from `accenture-prep-final/accenture-prep-comm-interview/interview/TECH-STACK-ANSWER.md`; verified in that library's QA, with the company name and evidence line changed.*

> **"What tech are you most comfortable with, and can you switch between tech stacks within a month?"**

Infosys's official eligibility text requires candidates to be “willing to work in different technologies as required by Infosys” `[CONFIRMED — official]`, and one 2025 interview asked “Java or Python, and why?” `[REPORTED]`. Expect the question in some form.

---

## 1. Why they ask it

Infosys is an IT services company. You will be staffed on a client project, and **you do not pick
the stack** — the client's existing system does. They are not testing whether you know their
technology. They are testing whether **putting you on something unfamiliar is a risk**.

The question has two halves and **the second half is the real one.** Most candidates answer the
first half well and the second half with a bare "yes, definitely, I'm a fast learner" — which is
exactly the answer everyone gives and therefore carries no information.

**The whole game is proving the second half with evidence instead of asserting it.**

---

## 2. Your answer

You write **Java**. Say so — plainly, without hedging. Then prove adaptability with things you have
actually done.

### The full answer, about 60 seconds

> "Java is where I'm most comfortable — that's what I reach for by default, and it's what I've used
> most for data structures and algorithms work. I'm also comfortable in Python and SQL, and I've
> built full-stack projects, so I've worked with JavaScript, React and TypeScript on the front end
> and FastAPI on the backend.
>
> On switching — I'd say yes, and I'd rather point at evidence than just assert it. My merged Zulip
> pull request was in TypeScript, and my Learning Unlimited contributions were in Python. Neither is
> my strongest language. What made those possible wasn't knowing the syntax, it was being able to
> read an unfamiliar codebase, find where the problem actually lived, and write a test that proved
> the fix worked. I'm also picking up Node.js at the moment.
>
> So a month to become productive in a new stack seems reasonable to me. Not expert — productive.
> The fundamentals carry over; it's the ecosystem and the conventions that take the time."

---

## 3. Why this answer works

| Move | Effect |
|---|---|
| Names Java plainly | Direct answers read as confident. Hedging reads as insecure |
| Lists the real range | Java, Python, SQL, JS/TS, React, FastAPI — all on your resume, all true |
| **Proves adaptability with merged PRs** | You have *already* shipped working code in two languages that are not your strongest. That is evidence, not a claim |
| Names what actually transfers | "Reading an unfamiliar codebase" is the real skill, and saying so shows you understand the job |
| **Distinguishes productive from expert** | The single most credible thing in the answer. It shows judgement, and it stops you overpromising |
| Mentions Node.js | Shows current, ongoing learning — and it is on your resume |

**The line that does the most work:** *"Not expert — productive."* Anyone can say "yes I can learn
anything in a month." Someone who defines what a month realistically buys sounds like they have
actually done it.

---

## 4. Follow-ups, and how to handle them

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **"So you don't know .NET / our stack?"** | "Not yet — I haven't worked in it. But my last two open-source contributions were in languages I hadn't used much either, and both got merged. I'd expect a ramp-up, and I'd expect to be useful inside a month." **Never pretend to know something you don't. It is trivially exposed.** |
| **"How would you actually learn a new stack?"** | "The way I did with TypeScript for the Zulip fix — get the project running locally first, read the existing code around the area I need to touch, make the smallest possible change, and write a test for it. Reading working code beats tutorials, because you see the conventions the project actually uses." |
| **"What if you're put on something you don't like?"** | "That's the job in services — I'd rather be on a real client project in an unfamiliar stack than an ideal stack with no real users. And I'd want to be honest with the lead about where my ramp-up is." |
| **"Java or Python — pick one."** | "Java, for anything structured and long-lived. Python when I'm working with ML or moving quickly — both my deployed projects have Python backends." **True, and it shows you choose by fit.** |
| **"Rate yourself out of 10 in Java."** | Avoid inflation. "Maybe a 7. Comfortable with the language, OOP and the standard library, and I can solve problems in it under time pressure. I haven't worked on a large production Java codebase, and I think that's the gap between a 7 and a 9." **Specific, honest, and it names the gap before they find it.** |
| **"Are you willing to work in support or testing rather than development?"** | Be honest, but understand Infosys staffs across many roles. If you are open, say so. If not, do not lie — it comes up again at allocation |

---

## 5. What not to say

| Don't | Why |
|---|---|
| *"I can learn anything in a week"* | Nobody believes it, and it suggests you do not know how long learning takes |
| *"I'm a quick learner"* with no evidence | The single most common empty sentence in interviews |
| *"I'm comfortable with everything"* | Reads as either dishonest or inexperienced |
| Listing 15 technologies | Invites a question about the one you know least |
| Naming a technology only on your resume as filler | If it is on the resume, you must be able to talk about it |

**The rule: everything you name, you must be able to answer one follow-up on.** Look at your own
resume and ask yourself that about every item on the skills line — especially **Docker (basics)**
and **Node.js (learning)**. Your resume already hedges both correctly, which is good. Keep the
hedge in the conversation too.

---

## 6. The one-line version

If the interview is short and you have fifteen seconds rather than sixty:

> "Java first, then Python and SQL. On switching — my merged open-source PRs were in TypeScript and
> Python, neither of which is my strongest language, so I've effectively done it already. A month to
> be productive, yes. A month to be expert, no."
