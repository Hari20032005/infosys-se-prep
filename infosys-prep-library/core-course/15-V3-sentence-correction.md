# Lesson 15. Sentence correction and completion

*Topic `V3` · about 3.2 of the 75 marks · plan about 1.0 hours.*

## 1. The idea

In a sentence correction question, part of a sentence is marked, and you choose the best way to write that part. One of the options is usually the original wording. If the original is already best, you pick it.

"Best" means two things at once. The option must be **correct** in grammar, and it must be **clear and short**. Of two correct options, the shorter and clearer one wins, as long as it keeps the same meaning.

So you use the same checklist as in the "correct sentence" lesson, and one more rule: **cut the words that add nothing**.

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

The official 2020 sample had four questions under the heading "English Error Correction" `[CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]`. Each marked a part of a sentence and gave four ways of writing it. The directions asked you to follow standard written English and to look at grammar, word choice and sentence building, and to choose the version that says the same thing most clearly and exactly, without awkward wording, vagueness or repeated ideas.

In practice the right answers did a few plain things: made a verb match its plural subject, removed an awkward phrase, joined a list in matching form, and replaced a long phrase with a short one.

A 2025 deck also lists "error corrections" and "sentence completion" under verbal ability `[REPORTED]`. The Verbal section is 20 questions in 20 minutes, about a minute each.

## 3. The method

1. **Read the whole sentence.** Do not read only the marked part.
2. **Find the job of the marked part.** Is it a verb, a pronoun, a list item, a joining phrase?
3. **Check it with the checklist:** agreement, tense, time words, pronouns, matching lists, the doer of an opening phrase, -ing after a preposition.
4. **If the marked part passes, the original is a strong candidate.** Do not change correct text just because the options look fancy.
5. **Compare the options that are correct.** Pick the one that is shortest and clearest, with the same meaning.
6. **Read your choice back inside the sentence.** Does it fit with the words after the marked part?

Rules to add to the checklist:

| Look for | The rule |
|---|---|
| Wordy phrases | "Due to the fact that" is "because". Cut words that add nothing. |
| Repeated meaning | Some pairs say the same thing twice: *past history*, *terrible tragedy*, *final outcome*, *free gift*, *completely finish*. Keep one. |
| Keep the meaning | The shorter option must say the same thing. Do not drop a needed word. |

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**The original is sometimes right.** One option usually repeats the marked wording, and it can be the answer. Do not let the other options tempt you. *It fails when the original has a clear error*, so always run the checklist.

**Look at what the options change.** If three options change the verb and one does not, the verb is probably the clue. *It fails when the clue is in the words outside the marked part.* Read the whole sentence.

**Shorter wins among equals.** Among correct options, the shorter one is usually best. *It fails when the short option changes the meaning.*

**Read the words after the marked part.** The option must join up with them. A verb that needs a plural subject must fit what follows. *It fails only for sentences with very long gaps*, so read slowly.

## 5. Worked examples

These are judgement questions, each explained with the rule.

**Example 1 (agreement).** "The list of items [are] on the desk."

The subject is *list*, which is singular. The words *of items* sit in between and do not change the verb. The best phrasing is **is**.

**Example 2 (cutting words).** "[In spite of the fact that] he was ill, he came to work."

The long phrase can be replaced by one word with the same meaning. The best phrasing is **Although**.

**Example 3 (repeated meaning).** "We are waiting for the [final outcome]."

An outcome is always final. The best phrasing is **outcome**. Purdue OWL lists *final outcome* among the pairs that say the same thing twice.

**Example 4 (matching list).** "The job needs someone who is patient, careful and [with experience]."

The first two items are single describing words. The third should be one too: **experienced**.

**Example 5 (no change).** "She [has lived] here since 2020."

Check it: *since 2020* with a state that is still true needs the present perfect. The original is correct. The best phrasing is **has lived**, the unchanged wording.

## 6. Traps

- **Changing correct text.** If the original passes the checklist, keep it.
- **Choosing the longest option because it sounds formal.** Longer is not better.
- **Dropping a needed word.** A shorter option that loses meaning is wrong.
- **Checking only the marked part.** Read the rest of the sentence. A verb has to match a subject that may be outside the brackets.
- **Fixing the grammar but leaving the repetition.** Two options may both be correct. The shorter, clearer one wins.

### ANSWER KEY

1. **a** — *Judgement:* The subject is *results*, which is plural, so the verb is **were published** (Purdue OWL, subject-verb agreement).
2. **c** — *Judgement:* *Since 2019* with a state that is still true needs the present perfect. The part is already correct, so keep **has worked** (Purdue OWL, prepositions of time; British Council, present perfect).
3. **b** — *Judgement:* *History* already means the past, so *past history* repeats itself. Purdue OWL lists *past history* among its redundant pairs. The best phrasing is **history**.
4. **d** — *Judgement:* *Information* is uncountable: no plural, no *an* (Purdue OWL, uncountable nouns). The best phrasing is **me the information**.
5. **d** — *Judgement:* Days take **on** (Purdue OWL, prepositions of time).
6. **a** — *Judgement:* *Neither* is singular, so the verb is **is** (Purdue OWL, subject-verb agreement).
7. **c** — *Judgement:* The first two items end in -ing, so the third must too: **speaking confidently** (Purdue OWL, parallel structure).
8. **b** — *Judgement:* A plural subject takes a plural pronoun, and the sentence stays in the third person: **their identity cards** (Purdue OWL, using pronouns clearly).
9. **b** — *Judgement:* *Tragedy* already means something terrible. Purdue OWL lists *terrible tragedy* among its redundant pairs. The best phrasing is **tragedy**.
10. **c** — *Judgement:* Two past events: leaving came first. The earlier event takes the past perfect, **had left** (Purdue OWL, active verb tenses).
11. **a** — *Judgement:* An opening phrase must name the person who did the action. *The error message* did not open the file, so the phrase must change; **When I opened the file** adds the doer (Purdue OWL, dangling modifiers).
12. **a** — *Judgement:* The subject is *quality*, which is singular, so **has improved**. *They* refers to the products (plural), so *they were launched* is right (Purdue OWL, a phrase between subject and verb).
13. **d** — *Judgement:* All four mean the same. The shortest phrase that does the job is **Because**; the others add empty words (Purdue OWL, concision).
14. **c** — *Judgement:* The list after *for* is made of -ing forms, so the last item must be an -ing form: **staying calm** (Purdue OWL, parallel structure).
15. **b** — *Judgement:* Here *to* is a preposition, not part of an infinitive, so the verb that follows takes its -ing form: **to meeting**. A gerund can follow a preposition (Purdue OWL, gerunds). Applying that to this idiom is my own step `[INFERRED]`.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- Read the whole sentence. Find the job of the marked part.
- Run the checklist: agreement, tense, time words, pronouns, lists, opening phrases, -ing after a preposition.
- Keep the original if it is right.
- Among correct options, pick the **shortest** that keeps the meaning.
- Cut empty phrases (*due to the fact that*) and repeated pairs (*final outcome*).
- Read your choice back into the sentence.
