# Lesson 17. Critical reasoning

*Topic `V6` · about 3.0 of the 75 marks · plan about 0.6 hours.*

## 1. The idea

A critical reasoning question gives you a short argument, which is a **conclusion** supported by **reasons**, and then asks you to do something with it. You may be asked what must be true, what would make it stronger, what would make it weaker, or what hidden step it relies on.

Every one of these uses the same picture. An argument is a bridge. The reasons are on one bank, the conclusion is on the other, and there is usually a gap in between. The gap is what the writer takes for granted without saying it.

- **Strengthen** means help fill the gap.
- **Weaken** means open the gap wider, often by offering another explanation.
- **Assumption** means name what must be true for the bridge to hold.
- **Inference** means say what follows for certain from the facts given.
- **Flaw** means name the gap.

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

The Verbal section in the official 2020 sample had three critical reasoning questions `[CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]`: one asked which statement **strengthens** an argument about foreign computer chips and missile accuracy, one asked which statement **weakens** an argument about a fuel blend, and one asked what can be **inferred** from facts about the health of two income groups. The answers were short and sat close to the argument.

No critical reasoning question has been reported from the 2025 papers `[REPORTED]`. The sample is the evidence. The Verbal section is 20 questions in 20 minutes, about a minute each.

## 3. The method

1. **Find the conclusion.** Words that often point to it: so, therefore, thus, should, hence. It is the point the rest of the text supports.
2. **Find the reasons.** These are the facts offered.
3. **Find the gap.** Ask: what must be true to get from these reasons to this conclusion?
4. **Read the question again.** Is it strengthen, weaken, assumption, inference or flaw?
5. **Test each option against the gap.**
    - Strengthen: does it fill the gap or add support?
    - Weaken: does it open the gap or offer another explanation?
    - Assumption: if it were false, would the argument fall apart?
    - Inference: does it follow with no extra step?
6. **Cross out options that are off the topic, or too strong.**

The test for an assumption: put "not" in front of it. If the argument then fails, the option is an assumption the argument needs.

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**Underline the conclusion first.** Many mistakes come from treating a reason as the conclusion. *It fails when the conclusion is not stated and has to be inferred.*

**For weaken, look for "another reason".** The usual weakener says something else could explain the facts. *It fails when the offered cause makes no difference to the result.*

**For inference, pick the safest option.** The answer is usually the one that says the least. *It fails when the safe option repeats the argument without adding anything required.*

**Extreme words are suspicious.** Options with "all", "never" and "only" go beyond the facts. *They fail when the argument itself talks about all or none.*

## 5. Worked examples

These are judgement questions. The reasoning is explained, because the point is the method.

**Example 1 (find the conclusion).** "The museum's visitors fell by a third after it began charging an entry fee. The fee should be removed."

The first sentence is a fact. The second says what should be done, so it is the conclusion.

**Example 2 (strengthen).** "The school should put solar panels on its roof, because it pays high electricity bills." Which fact helps most?

*The roof gets full sun for most of the day.* The gap is whether panels would work well and cut the bills. Full sun fills it. A fact about the colour of the roof does not.

**Example 3 (weaken).** "Readers prefer paper books, because the bookshop sells many more paper books than e-books." Which fact hurts most?

*The shop sells e-books only on special order.* If people cannot easily buy e-books there, the sales figures do not show a preference.

**Example 4 (assumption).** "Rahul will get the job, since he has the best degree of all the applicants." What must be assumed?

*The job will go to the person with the best degree.* Test it with "not": if the job is given on some other basis, the argument fails.

**Example 5 (flaw).** "Every successful start-up works long hours. Meena works long hours. So Meena's start-up will be successful." What is wrong?

The first sentence says success comes with long hours. It does not say long hours bring success, so many people who work long hours may still fail. The argument reverses the statement.

## 6. Traps

- **Picking an option because it is true in real life.** It must matter to this argument.
- **Confusing strengthen with weaken.** Read the question twice.
- **Choosing "too strong" options.** If the option claims more than the facts, it is probably wrong for an inference question.
- **Taking a reason as the conclusion.** Find the "should" or "so".
- **Forgetting the "not" test.** It is the quickest check for an assumption.


## 7. Practice questions

Attempt each level before opening the key. Level 3 is harder than anything in the 2020 official sample, on purpose: if you can do Level 3, the real paper will feel easy.

### Level 1: warm-up

*Spotting the conclusion, a simple inference, and easy strengthen and weaken questions.*

**1.** **[Conclusion]** > The canteen raised its prices last month. Since then, fewer staff eat there, and the canteen's income has fallen. The canteen should lower its prices again.  

Which sentence is the main conclusion of the argument?
a) Fewer staff eat in the canteen.  
b) The canteen's income has fallen.  
c) The canteen should lower its prices again.  
d) The canteen raised its prices last month.  

**2.** **[Inference]** > All the trains on the Chennai line stop at Katpadi. This train is on the Chennai line.  

Which statement must be true?
a) This train starts at Katpadi.  
b) This train stops at Katpadi.  
c) Only trains on the Chennai line stop at Katpadi.  
d) Every train that stops at Katpadi is on the Chennai line.  

**3.** **[Inference]** > In a survey of 200 students, 150 said they use the college library at least once a week.  

Which statement must be true?
a) 75 percent of all students in the country use their library weekly.  
b) Most students in the college use the library every day.  
c) At least 150 of the students surveyed use the library at least once a week.  
d) Fifty of the students never use the library.  

**4.** **[Strengthen]** > A shop plans to stay open until 9 p.m. because it believes evening customers will raise its sales.  

Which fact most strengthens the shop's plan?
a) Other shops in the area close at 6 p.m.  
b) The shop owner lives nearby.  
c) The shop's lights are old and use a lot of power.  
d) Many people in the area finish work after 6 p.m. and buy groceries on their way home.  

**5.** **[Weaken]** > App X is better than App Y, because App X has been downloaded many more times.  

Which fact most weakens this argument?
a) App X is free to download.  
b) App X comes pre-installed on most new phones, so many downloads happen automatically.  
c) Both apps have a blue icon.  
d) App Y was launched one year ago.  

### Level 2: exam level

*Strengthening and weakening cause-and-effect arguments, an assumption and a flaw about a missing comparison.*

**6.** **[Strengthen]** > A school added a ten-minute walk after lunch. Within a term, afternoon test scores rose. The principal concludes that the walk raised the scores.  

Which fact most strengthens the principal's conclusion?
a) Afternoon scores in classes of the same school that did not take the walk stayed the same.  
b) The walk was popular with the teachers.  
c) Test scores were also high in the morning.  
d) The school has a large playground.  

**7.** **[Weaken]** > A school added a ten-minute walk after lunch. Within a term, afternoon test scores rose. The principal concludes that the walk raised the scores.  

Which fact most weakens the principal's conclusion?
a) The walk takes place on the school grounds.  
b) In the same term the school also began extra afternoon tutoring for every class.  
c) Some students walked faster than others.  
d) The principal supports the walk.  

**8.** **[Assumption]** > The market street should be closed to cars, because it is noisy and crowded with traffic.  

The argument depends on which assumption?
a) Cars are the main reason people visit the market.  
b) Most shoppers arrive by bus.  
c) The market is the oldest in the city.  
d) Closing the street to cars would reduce the noise and the crowding.  

**9.** **[Inference]** > Every parcel sent by Express is delivered within two days. Anu's parcel was delivered in four days.  

What can be concluded?
a) Anu's parcel was not sent by Express.  
b) Anu's parcel was lost.  
c) Express is slower than other services.  
d) Anu paid too little.  

**10.** **[Weaken]** > A café notes that on days when it sells more iced coffee, its sales of salads also rise. The owner decides that iced coffee makes customers buy salads.  

Which fact most weakens the owner's decision?
a) Salads are made fresh each morning.  
b) The café sells soup in winter.  
c) Iced coffee costs more than salads.  
d) Both iced coffee and salad sales rise on hot days, when more people visit.  

### Level 3: beyond the exam

*Necessary assumptions, flaws that reverse a statement or confuse correlation with cause, evaluate questions and number inferences.*

**11.** **[Assumption]** > The city should build a second bridge, because the existing bridge is crowded every morning.  

Which assumption does the argument NEED to be reasonable?
a) The crowding would not simply move to the new bridge and stay as bad.  
b) The existing bridge was built after 1990.  
c) Every morning commuter wants a new bridge.  
d) A second bridge would be free to build.  

**12.** **[Flaw]** > All members of the chess club are good at maths. Priya is good at maths. So Priya is a member of the chess club.  

What is the flaw in this argument?
a) It uses a survey that is too small.  
b) It assumes Priya plays chess well.  
c) Being good at maths is not shown to be enough to be a member; others may be good at maths too.  
d) It argues in a circle.  

**13.** **[Flaw]** > In seaside towns, ice cream sales and drowning cases both go up in the same months. A columnist says that eating ice cream causes drowning.  

What is the flaw in the columnist's reasoning?
a) Both may be caused by something else, such as hot weather that brings crowds to the beach.  
b) It assumes people like ice cream.  
c) It uses too many numbers.  
d) It ignores that some towns have no sea.  

**14.** **[Evaluate]** > A company says its new training programme raises sales: staff who took it sold 12 percent more than they did before.  

Which question would be most useful in judging the company's claim?
a) Do the trainers have degrees?  
b) Did staff who did not take the training also sell more over the same period?  
c) Who designed the training programme?  
d) How long was each training session?  

**15.** **[Inference]** > In a hostel of 80 students, every student who plays cricket also plays football. Thirty students play football.  

Which statement must be true?
a) At least 30 students play cricket.  
b) Exactly 30 students play cricket.  
c) At most 30 students play cricket.  
d) 50 students play neither game.  

### ANSWER KEY

1. **c** — *Judgement:* A conclusion is the point the other sentences support. The first three are reasons or facts; the last one says what should be done.
2. **b** — *Judgement:* Every train on the line stops there, and this train is on the line. The other options claim more than the facts say.
3. **c** — *Judgement:* 150 of the 200 surveyed said so. Nothing is said about other students, about daily use, or about the other 50 (they may use it less than weekly).
4. **d** — *Judgement:* A fact that shows customers exist in the evening supports the belief. The other options do not show that anyone would buy.
5. **b** — *Judgement:* If most downloads are automatic, the count says little about whether people prefer App X. The other options do not touch the reasoning.
6. **a** — *Judgement:* A comparison group that did not walk and did not improve shows the rise is more likely due to the walk (this is what a good test needs).
7. **b** — *Judgement:* If another change (extra tutoring) started at the same time, it offers a different explanation for the rise.
8. **d** — *Judgement:* Without the belief that closing the street would ease the noise and crowding, the reason given would not lead to the conclusion.
9. **a** — *Judgement:* If Express always delivers in two days, a parcel that took four days cannot have been sent by Express.
10. **d** — *Judgement:* A third factor (hot weather) could explain both rises, so one need not cause the other.
11. **a** — *Judgement:* The plan only helps if a new bridge actually eases the crowding. The other options are not needed for that.
12. **c** — *Judgement:* The first statement says club members are good at maths, not that everyone good at maths is a member. The conclusion reverses it.
13. **a** — *Judgement:* Two things rising together does not show one causes the other; a common cause explains the pattern more simply.
14. **b** — *Judgement:* If untrained staff also sold more, the rise may have another cause (a busy season), so the training is not shown to matter.
15. **c** — *Judgement:* Cricket players are all inside the group of footballers, so they cannot be more than 30. Nothing says how many there are, and some of the other 50 may play other games.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- Argument = reasons + conclusion, with a gap in between.
- Find the conclusion first (so, therefore, should).
- Strengthen fills the gap. Weaken opens it with another explanation. Assumption: what must be true (test with "not"). Inference: what must follow.
- Cross out off-topic and too-strong options.
- "Two things rise together" does not mean one causes the other.
