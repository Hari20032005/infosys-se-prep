# Lesson 18. The essay and the email

*Topic `W1` · a pass/fail gate · plan about 1.0 hours.*

## 1. The idea

The writing task is one short piece, an essay or a formal email, written in about ten minutes. You are not judged as a poet. You are judged on whether the piece is clear, in order, and free of silly mistakes.

A clear piece has a simple shape.

- **An essay** has an opening that states your point, a few short paragraphs that support it with specifics, and a closing that returns to the point.
- **A formal email** has a subject line, a polite greeting, a first line that says why you are writing, the details, a clear request, thanks, and a closing with your name.

If you memorise these two shapes, you never start from a blank page. You only fill them.

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

One candidate who sat the on-campus test on 12 May 2025 reported an essay on "Ozone layer depletion" `[REPORTED]`. Coaches say the task is one essay or a formal email on a general topic, in about ten minutes, and aim for 190 to 200 words `[REPORTED]`.

The English Writing section is the last of the seven. It carries its own cut-off, but its marks have not been published `[LIKELY]`. That is why this lesson is short but worth doing: it is a gate, so a weak piece can cost you the whole test, and an hour of practice is cheap insurance against obvious mistakes.

Ten minutes means a rhythm of about two minutes planning, six minutes writing and two minutes checking. Six minutes of writing at about 32 words a minute gives roughly 190 words.

## 3. The method

For an essay:

1. **Spend two minutes planning.** Write your point in one sentence. List three supporting points, each with one fact or example.
2. **Write the opening.** Two or three sentences. Say what the topic is and give your point.
3. **Write three short paragraphs**, one for each supporting point. Start each with a sentence that names the point, then give a specific fact.
4. **Write the close.** Return to your point and end with a thought for the future or an action.
5. **Check for two minutes.** Look for spelling, missing full stops and sentences that run on.

For an email:

1. **Subject line:** say what the email is about.
2. **Greeting:** "Dear Mr Rao," followed by a comma.
3. **First line:** why you are writing.
4. **Details:** the facts the reader needs, in short paragraphs.
5. **Request:** say exactly what you want.
6. **Thanks and close:** thank the reader, then "Yours sincerely," and your name.

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**A fixed template.** Keep the five-paragraph shape in your head: opening, two or three supports, close. *It fails when the topic asks for two sides*, such as "online or classroom learning". Then give one paragraph for each side.

**Start with the thesis.** One or two sentences that answer the question. This is the advice given for timed exam essays. *It fails if you write a story first.*

**Specifics beat adjectives.** "Many students" is weaker than "thirty students in a class of fifty". *It fails if you invent facts you are not sure of.* Use ones you know.

**Short sentences.** Aim for 12 to 18 words. Short sentences lower your risk of errors. *They fail if every sentence is the same length*, so vary them a little.

## 5. Worked examples

All the word counts were checked by code, and the facts in the essay were checked against the US Environmental Protection Agency and the UN Ozone Secretariat.

**Example 1 (a model essay, 196 words).** Topic: ozone layer depletion.

> The ozone layer is a thin shield of gas high above the Earth. It absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet rays, so it protects every form of life. Sadly, human activity has damaged this shield, and the damage is a serious problem for all of us.
>
> The main cause is a group of chemicals called CFCs. For years they were used in refrigerators, air conditioners and aerosol sprays. When they escape, they rise into the upper atmosphere. Strong sunlight breaks them down, and the chlorine they release destroys ozone.
>
> The effects are dangerous. With less ozone, more ultraviolet light reaches the ground. This can cause skin cancer and cataracts in people, and it can harm crops and sea life. These effects are why scientists watch the ozone layer closely.
>
> The world has already taken action. In 1987, countries signed the Montreal Protocol to phase out the chemicals that destroy ozone. This shows that nations can work together when the danger is clear.
>
> We can also help. We can choose products that do not harm the ozone layer, repair appliances properly, and teach others about the problem. Protecting the ozone layer is protecting our own future.

Look at the shape. Paragraph 1 states the point. Paragraphs 2, 3 and 4 give the cause, the effects and the action. Paragraph 5 returns to the point. Every claim has a specific fact behind it.

**Example 2 (a model email, 102 words).** Task: ask a training manager to move you to a later batch because of an exam.

> Subject: Request to move my training batch
>
> Dear Mr Rao,
>
> I hope you are well. I am writing about my training batch. I have been placed in Batch B, which starts on 5 January. Unfortunately, I have a university examination on 8 January that I cannot miss.
>
> I therefore request you to move me to Batch C, which starts on 15 January. I can send a copy of the examination timetable if you need it. I will also let my team lead know about this change.
>
> Thank you for considering my request. I look forward to your reply.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Arjun Menon

Look at the order. There is a subject line, a greeting, the reason, the details, a clear request, thanks, and a closing.

**Example 3 (a thesis).** Weak: "Pollution is bad." Strong: "Air pollution harms health and should be cut by stricter rules." The strong version takes a position and can be supported.

**Example 4 (a subject line).** Weak: "Leave." Strong: "Request for two days' leave, 12 and 13 March." The strong one tells the reader what the email is about.

**Example 5 (cutting words).** Weak: "Due to the fact that it was raining, the match was stopped." Strong: "Because it was raining, the match was stopped." Same meaning, fewer words.

**Prompts to practise (10 minutes each).**

- Essay: "Plastic waste in our cities."
- Essay: "Social media: a help or a hindrance for students?"
- Email: ask the HR team to move your joining date by one week because of a family event.

## 6. Traps

- **Starting with "In today's world".** It says nothing. Begin with your point.
- **Writing a story instead of an argument.** Keep to the shape.
- **Using text language in an email.** "U" for "you" and "asap" count against you.
- **Running out of time before the close.** Plan the close in advance.
- **Skipping the check.** Two minutes find the missing full stops and spelling slips.


## 7. Practice questions

Attempt each level before opening the key. Level 3 is harder than anything in the 2020 official sample, on purpose: if you can do Level 3, the real paper will feel easy.

### Level 1: warm-up

*Parts of an email, and the start of an essay.*

**1.** **[Subject line]** You must write to your manager to ask for two days of leave. Which subject line is best?  
a) Request for two days' leave, 12 and 13 March  
b) URGENT!!! PLEASE READ  
c) Leave  
d) Hello  

**2.** **[Greeting]** You are writing to Mr Rao, a training manager you have not met. Which greeting is best?  
a) Hi everyone  
b) Hey Rao  
c) Dear Mr Rao,  
d) Dear friend  

**3.** **[Closing]** Which closing is best for the same email?  
a) Bye for now, Arjun  
b) Yours sincerely, Arjun Menon  
c) See you!! Arjun  
d) Cheers, Arjun  

**4.** **[Tone]** Which sentence suits a formal email?  
a) U need to confirm the date.  
b) Gimme the date.  
c) Tell me the date asap.  
d) I would be grateful if you could confirm the date.  

**5.** **[Opening]** An essay answer should begin with  
a) a one-sentence thesis that answers the question  
b) a long story about your day  
c) a list of every point you know  
d) a quotation with no explanation  

### Level 2: exam level

*Thesis, topic and closing sentences, one sentence that does not belong, and a joining word.*

**6.** **[Thesis]** Essay question: "Is online learning better than classroom learning?" Which opening sentence is the clearest thesis?  
a) Many people have different opinions about learning.  
b) Online learning is more flexible, but classroom learning is better for building habits and friendships.  
c) I will now write about learning.  
d) Learning is important.  

**7.** **[Unity]** Which sentence does NOT belong in this paragraph?  

(1) Vehicle smoke is a major cause of air pollution. (2) Factories also release harmful gases. (3) The city's new library has a large reading hall. (4) Burning waste in open spaces adds to the problem.
a) (4)  
b) (2)  
c) (3)  
d) (1)  

**8.** **[Topic sentence]** Which is the best topic sentence for a paragraph that lists three causes of stress among students?  
a) Stress.  
b) Students face stress from exams, long hours and family expectations.  
c) Many things happen in life.  
d) I am a student.  

**9.** **[Conclusion]** Which is the best final sentence for an essay arguing that recycling saves resources?  
a) That is all I know about recycling.  
b) Recycling is a topic.  
c) Recycling is a small habit that saves resources for the future, and everyone should adopt it.  
d) Another thing about recycling is plastic bottles.  

**10.** **[Transition]** The factory reduced its smoke. ___, the air in the town is cleaner.  
a) For example  
b) On the other hand  
c) However  
d) As a result  

### Level 3: beyond the exam

*Concise wording, the order of an email, unclear pronouns, a time plan and supporting evidence.*

**11.** **[Concision]** Which version is the most concise without losing meaning?  
a) Should it be the case that it rains, the match will be cancelled.  
b) In the event that it rains, the match will be cancelled.  
c) If it rains, the match will be cancelled.  
d) In the event of the situation where it rains, the match will be cancelled.  

**12.** **[Order]** Put the parts of a formal request email in the best order: A greeting, B reason for writing, C details, D request and thanks, E closing and name.  
a) B, A, C, E, D  
b) A, C, B, E, D  
c) E, A, B, C, D  
d) A, B, C, D, E  

**13.** **[Clear pronoun]** Which sentence removes the unclear pronoun in "The manager told the engineer that he would get a bonus"?  
a) The manager said to the engineer, "You will get a bonus."  
b) The manager and the engineer got a bonus when he said so.  
c) The manager told the engineer that he would get a bonus soon.  
d) It was said that he would get a bonus.  

**14.** **[Time]** You have 10 minutes for a 190-word essay. Which plan is best?  
a) Spend 5 minutes choosing a title  
b) 2 minutes planning, 6 minutes writing, 2 minutes checking  
c) Write the conclusion first and then stop  
d) 9 minutes writing, 1 minute thinking  

**15.** **[Support]** Which sentence best supports the claim "Reading every day improves vocabulary"?  
a) A student who reads for thirty minutes a day meets hundreds of new words in a month.  
b) Everyone knows reading is good.  
c) Many people read.  
d) Reading is fun and relaxing.  

### ANSWER KEY

1. **a** — *Judgement:* A meaningful subject line tells the reader what the email is about (Purdue OWL, email etiquette).
2. **c** — *Judgement:* Open a formal email with a greeting such as *Dear Dr. Jones,*, followed by a comma (Purdue OWL, email etiquette; British Council, starting and finishing emails).
3. **b** — *Judgement:* Formal closings listed by the British Council include *Yours sincerely,* followed by the name. The other three are friendly and informal (Purdue OWL: a sign-off phrase and the sender's name).
4. **d** — *Judgement:* Use standard spelling and no text abbreviations such as *u* (Purdue OWL, email etiquette). The first option is also polite.
5. **a** — *Judgement:* Begin with a one or two sentence thesis that restates the question as a statement (Purdue OWL, essays for exams).
6. **b** — *Judgement:* A thesis takes a position that answers the question. The other openings only name the topic or announce what is coming (Purdue OWL, essays for exams).
7. **c** — *Judgement:* Sentences 1, 2 and 4 are causes of air pollution. Sentence 3 is about a library, so it breaks the paragraph's single idea.
8. **b** — *Judgement:* A topic sentence states the paragraph's one idea and hints at its three parts.
9. **c** — *Judgement:* A conclusion returns to the main point and closes it. The others add a new point, apologise, or say nothing.
10. **d** — *Judgement:* The cleaner air follows from the reduced smoke, so a word of result is needed: **As a result**.
11. **c** — *Judgement:* All four mean the same, so the shortest is best (Purdue OWL, concision).
12. **d** — *Judgement:* Greet first, say why you are writing, give the details, make the request and thank the reader, then close with your name.
13. **a** — *Judgement:* In the original, *he* could be the manager or the engineer. Quoting the words ends the doubt (Purdue OWL, using pronouns clearly: refer to a specific noun).
14. **b** — *Judgement:* Budget your time, plan, write and proofread (Purdue OWL, essays for exams). Six minutes is enough for about 190 words if you have planned.
15. **a** — *Judgement:* Support a point with specific evidence, not a general statement (Purdue OWL, essays for exams).

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- Essay: point, then three supported paragraphs, then close. About 190 words.
- Email: subject, greeting, why, details, request, thanks, close with your name.
- Plan 2 minutes, write 6, check 2.
- Specifics beat adjectives. Short sentences. Cut empty words.
- No text language. No "In today's world".
