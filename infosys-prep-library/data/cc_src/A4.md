## 1. The idea

A syllogism question gives you two statements about groups of things, and then two conclusions. You decide which conclusions follow.

"Follows" has a strict meaning. A conclusion follows only if it is **true in every possible picture** that matches the statements. It is not enough that it is true in the picture you drew first. The test is always: can I draw a picture that keeps the statements true and makes the conclusion false? If yes, it does not follow.

One agreement keeps things simple. Every group that is named is assumed to have at least one member. That is the usual rule in these exams, and this lesson uses it.

## 3. The method

1. **Turn each statement into a picture rule.**
    - "All A are B": the A circle sits inside the B circle.
    - "Some A are B": the circles overlap somewhere.
    - "No A are B": the circles are apart.
    - "Some A are not B": part of A sits outside B.
2. **Draw a picture that keeps both statements true.**
3. **Try to break each conclusion.** Move circles, within what the statements allow, until the conclusion is false. If you can, it does not follow.
4. **Keep only the conclusions you could not break.**
5. **Check for "either / or".** If the two conclusions are opposites (one says "Some A are B" and the other says "No A are B"), and neither follows alone, then one of them must be true. The answer is "either I or II follows".

The standard results, for statements joining X to Y and Y to Z, found by trying every possible picture:

| Statements | What follows between X and Z |
|---|---|
| All X are Y, all Y are Z | All X are Z; some X are Z |
| All X are Y, some Y are Z | Nothing |
| Some X are Y, all Y are Z | Some X are Z |
| All X are Y, no Y are Z | No X are Z; some X are not Z; some Z are not X |
| Some X are Y, no Y are Z | Some X are not Z |
| No X are Y, all Y are Z | Some Z are not X |
| Some X are Y, some Y are Z | Nothing |
| Some X are not Y, all Y are Z | Nothing |

"Some X are Z" and "Some Z are X" mean the same. So do "No X are Z" and "No Z are X".

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**Two "some" statements give nothing.** If both statements start with "some", nothing links X and Z. *It fails if a third statement is added.* Then redraw.

**"All" then "all" chains.** All X are Y and all Y are Z means all X are Z. Use this at once. *It fails if the second statement runs the other way* (all Z are Y). Then you only know X and Z both sit inside Y.

**A "some" in the conclusion needs a "some" or a nesting.** To get "Some X are Z" you need an overlap that is certain. An "all X are Y" with "all Y are Z" gives a certain overlap, because the groups are not empty. *It fails if the overlap rests on only one "some" in the wrong place.*

**Watch the opposites for "either / or".** Two conclusions that deny each other, with neither certain, give "either". *It fails when the conclusions are about different groups.*

## 5. Worked examples

All the answers below were found by trying every possible arrangement in code, using two different search methods that agreed.

**Example 1 (both follow).** Statements: All doctors are graduates. All graduates are readers. Conclusions: I. All doctors are readers. II. Some readers are doctors.

All inside all gives I. Because doctors exist, some readers are doctors, which is II. Answer: **both follow**.

**Example 2 (neither).** Statements: All cats are pets. Some pets are animals. Conclusions: I. Some cats are animals. II. All animals are pets.

The pets may overlap animals without touching any cat, so I can be false. The animals may be bigger than pets, so II can be false. Answer: **neither follows**.

**Example 3 (only I).** Statements: Some roses are flowers. All flowers are plants. Conclusions: I. Some roses are plants. II. All roses are plants.

The roses that are flowers are plants, so I follows. Some roses may not be flowers at all, so II can be false. Answer: **only I follows**.

**Example 4 (either or).** Statements: All poets are writers. Some writers are artists. Conclusions: I. Some poets are artists. II. No poet is an artist.

The poets might overlap the artists or might not. So neither conclusion follows alone. But they are opposites: one must be true. Answer: **either I or II follows**.

**Example 5 (the official-sample kind).** Five statements; choose the three where the first two lead to the third. Take these three: A. All teachers are adults. B. Some teachers are citizens. C. Some adults are citizens.

A and B together give C: the teachers who are citizens are adults, so some adults are citizens. A alone does not give C. B alone does not give C. So **ABC** works, in that order.

## 6. Traps

- **Using what you know about the real world.** Treat the statements as the only truth. A "pet" can be anything the statement says.
- **Checking only one picture.** Draw the awkward ones too, such as circles that only just touch.
- **Treating "some" as "most".** "Some" means at least one, possibly all.
- **Forgetting "either / or".** It only works when the conclusions are opposites and neither follows alone.
- **Reading "some A are not B" as "no A are B".** The first says part of A is outside B. The second says all of A is.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- A conclusion **follows** only if it is true in every picture that fits the statements.
- Picture rules: All = inside. Some = overlap. No = apart. Some not = part outside.
- Two "some" statements: nothing follows. All + all: all (and some).
- Opposite conclusions, neither certain alone: **either I or II**.
- Assume every named group has at least one member.
- Official set: pick three where the first two lead to the third.
