## 1. The idea

In a reading comprehension question, you read a short passage and answer questions about it. The answer is always in the passage, or follows directly from it. You are not tested on what you know about the world.

Treat the passage as the only source of truth. If an option sounds right but the passage does not say it, it is wrong. If an option sounds odd but the passage says it, it is right.

Most questions fall into five kinds:

- **A fact.** The passage states it. Find the sentence.
- **The main idea.** What is the whole passage about?
- **An inference.** Something that must be true because of what the passage says.
- **A word in context.** What does a word mean in this sentence?
- **The writer's attitude.** Is the writer for, against, or neutral?

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

The official 2020 sample had one passage with five questions, about the plays of a famous writer `[CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]`. Four of the five were about facts stated in the passage: what was noted about the techniques, what the plays are about, which statement matches the passage, and which play is named for a certain joke. The fifth asked why the plays are treasured, and the answer was in the closing sentence.

So the sample was mostly about **finding the sentence that says it**. The passage was longer than the ones here, but the skills are the same.

The Verbal section is 20 questions in 20 minutes. A passage with five questions is a quarter of the section, and each question gets about a minute.

## 3. The method

1. **Read the passage once, fast.** Note the first sentence, the last sentence, and what each paragraph is about.
2. **Read the question and all four options** before you go back to the passage.
3. **Find the lines that answer it.** Put a finger on them.
4. **Match the options against those lines.** Cross out any option that says something the lines do not say, or says more than they do.
5. **For an inference, ask: must this be true?** If it only might be true, it is not the answer.
6. **For a word in context, put each option in the sentence.** The one that keeps the meaning is the answer.

Traps to scan for in the options:

| Trap | Example |
|---|---|
| Too strong | "always", "never", "all", "only" when the passage says "many", "often" |
| Not in the passage | A true fact about the world that the passage does not mention |
| Half right | The first half matches, the second half does not |
| Wrong person or number | A number or a name taken from a different sentence |

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**The first and last sentences.** They often give the main idea and the writer's view. *They fail when the passage opens with a story or a quotation.* Read the middle too.

**Look for the contrast word.** "However", "but" and "yet" mark a turn. The writer's view is usually after the turn. *It fails when the passage has several turns.* Follow the last one.

**Cross out the extremes first.** Options with "always" or "never" are usually wrong. *They fail when the passage itself uses a strong word.*

**Skim, then search.** Do not memorise the passage. Know where things are. *It fails for inference questions*, which need the whole passage in mind.

## 5. Worked examples

Use this short passage.

> The old railway station at Mandya closed in 2012, and for years its hall stood empty. Last spring a group of local artists asked the town to let them use it. They painted the walls, hung lamps from the roof beams, and began to hold a market there every Sunday. Within months, the market had sixty stalls. Not everyone is pleased: some shopkeepers on the main road say they have lost customers. The artists reply that the market brings more visitors to the town, and that the shopkeepers will gain in the end.

These are judgement questions, each explained with the lines it rests on.

**Example 1 (a fact).** When did the station close? **2012.** The first sentence says so.

**Example 2 (an inference).** What can be inferred about the market? **It attracted many sellers quickly.** The market had sixty stalls "within months". The passage never says how many people visit, so "it is crowded every day" would be too strong.

**Example 3 (the main idea).** Which sentence is the best summary? **A disused station became a market, which pleases some people and worries others.** It covers the empty hall, the artists, and the shopkeepers' complaint. A summary that mentions only the artists leaves out half of the passage.

**Example 4 (a word in context).** In "its hall stood empty", what does "stood" mean? **Remained.** Try "was lifted up" and "was upright": neither fits a hall that was unused for years. "Remained empty" fits.

**Example 5 (the writer's attitude).** What is the writer's tone? **Balanced.** The writer reports what the artists did, what the shopkeepers said, and what the artists replied. There is no word that favours one side.

## 6. Traps

- **Choosing what you know.** A fact about railways that the passage does not mention is not an answer.
- **Choosing the first option that sounds right.** Read all four.
- **Mixing up speakers.** The shopkeepers complain, and the artists reply. Check who said what.
- **Missing the "NOT" in a question.** Underline it before you read the options.
- **Going back too late.** If you are unsure, return to the lines at once, not after you have talked yourself into an answer.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- The passage is the only source of truth.
- Read once for the gist, read the question, find the lines, match the options.
- Inference means **must be true**, not might be true.
- Word in context: put each option into the sentence.
- Watch for "always", "never", "all" and "only", and for the "NOT" in a question.
