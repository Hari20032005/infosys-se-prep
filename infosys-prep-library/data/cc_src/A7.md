## 1. The idea

Data interpretation means reading numbers from a table, a chart or a paragraph and answering a question with them. The maths is plain: percentages, ratios and averages. The work is in picking the right numbers and the right base.

Almost every question is one of five calculations:

- **A share.** What percent of the total is this part? Part divided by whole, times 100.
- **A change.** By what percent did it rise or fall? Change divided by the **starting** value, times 100.
- **A ratio.** How does one number compare with another? Write them as a pair and simplify.
- **An average.** Add the numbers and divide by how many there are.
- **A conversion.** A pie chart gives shares. Turn each share into a number by multiplying by the total.

If you know which of the five a question is, you already know the formula.

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

Two data interpretation questions were found in the 2025 reports `[REPORTED]`:

- A table of the heights of 400 students in five groups, asking what percent are shorter than 170 cm. The answer was 77%.
- A student's total of 400 marks split into five subjects by percentage, with a pass mark, asking which subject failed and the gap between the best and the worst. The answer was Science, with a gap of 96.

Three coaches said data interpretation appears in nearly every paper `[REPORTED]`. That is a coaching claim, not a measured fact.

Be careful with the name. In the official 2020 sample, the heading "Data Interpretation" was used for a logic puzzle about a thief, three computers, three tools and three months `[CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]`. It had no chart. So expect both kinds under the same heading.

## 3. The method

1. **Read the question first.** Underline what you must find: a share, a change, a ratio, an average.
2. **Take only the numbers you need** from the table. Ignore the rest.
3. **Write the formula in one line**, and put the numbers in.
4. **Check the base.** For a change, the base is the starting year. For a share, the base is the whole.
5. **Calculate, then sanity-check.** Is a 25% rise of 400 about 500? Yes. If your answer is 800, something is wrong.

Formulas to keep in your head:

| Question | Formula |
|---|---|
| Share of the total | part ÷ whole × 100 |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ **old** × 100 |
| Ratio | first : second, divided by their common factor |
| Average | total ÷ how many |
| From a pie share to a number | share × total ÷ 100 |

Fractions worth knowing as percentages: 1/2 is 50%, 1/3 is 33.33%, 1/4 is 25%, 1/5 is 20%, 1/6 is 16.67%, 1/8 is 12.5%, 1/10 is 10% and 1/12 is 8.33%.

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**Use the fractions.** If a share is close to 1/4 or 1/5 of the total, compare options with 25% or 20% before you calculate. *It fails when two options are close together.*

**Compare ratios without converting.** To find the greatest rise, divide each year by the one before. The largest division is the largest percentage rise. *It fails if you divide the wrong way.*

**Add the percentages in a pie.** A pie chart always adds to 100%. If one slice is missing, it is 100 minus the others. *It fails with a chart that shows only part of the whole.*

**Round and compare.** For "which is larger" questions, round to the nearest ten and compare. *It fails when the numbers differ by only a few percent.*

## 5. Worked examples

All the numbers below were checked by code. Use this table for Examples 1, 2 and 4.

| Year | Plant A | Plant B |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 400 | 350 |
| 2020 | 500 | 400 |
| 2021 | 450 | 600 |

**Example 1 (percentage change).** By what percent did Plant A's output change from 2019 to 2020?

Change: 500 − 400 = 100. Divide by the starting year: 100 ÷ 400 = 0.25. Answer: **25%**.

**Example 2 (ratio).** What is the ratio of A to B in 2021?

450 : 600. Divide both by 150. Answer: **3 : 4**.

**Example 3 (a pie chart as a table).** A student scored 500 marks in total. The shares are English 30%, Hindi 25%, Maths 20%, Science 15% and History 10%. The pass mark in each subject is 60. Which subject did the student fail, and what is the gap between the best and the worst?

Turn each share into marks: 150, 125, 100, 75 and 50. Only History (50) is under 60. The gap: 150 − 50 = **100**. Answer: **History, 100**.

**Example 4 (a combined total, and a trap).** By what percent did the **combined** output of both plants change from 2019 to 2020?

2019: 400 + 350 = 750. 2020: 500 + 400 = 900. Change: 150. Divide by 750: 0.2. Answer: **20%**. A alone rose 25% and B alone rose about 14.29%. The combined figure is not the average of those two.

**Example 5 (a frequency table).** Marks of 100 students: 0 to 20, 5 students; 20 to 40, 15; 40 to 60, 40; 60 to 80, 30; 80 to 100, 10. What percent scored below 60?

Add the first three groups: 5 + 15 + 40 = 60. Out of 100, that is **60%**.

## 6. Traps

- **The wrong base.** A fall from 500 to 400 is 20% (100 out of 500). A rise from 400 to 500 is 25% (100 out of 400). The same gap gives different percentages.
- **Percent and percentage points.** If a share goes from 20% to 25%, that is 5 percentage points, and a 25% rise.
- **Averaging averages.** The combined percent change is not the average of two percent changes. Work from the totals.
- **Reading the wrong row or column.** Put a finger on the year and the plant before you read the number.
- **Using the whole table.** Most questions need two numbers. Do not add up the rest.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- Five calculations: share, change, ratio, average, pie conversion.
- Change is over the **old** value. Share is over the **whole**.
- Take only the numbers you need. Sanity-check the size of the answer.
- Know the fractions: 1/8 is 12.5%, 1/6 is 16.67%, 1/3 is 33.33%.
- Combined percentages come from the totals, not from averaging percentages.
