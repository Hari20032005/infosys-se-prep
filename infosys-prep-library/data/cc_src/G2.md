## 1. The idea

A cloze test is a short passage with gaps. Each gap has four choices. You pick the word that makes the passage correct and sensible. It is the fill-in-the-blank skill from Lesson 12, applied across a whole paragraph.

The gaps are not random. Each one tests one grammar point: a **tense**, an **article** (a, an, the), a **preposition** (in, on, at, to), a **connecting word** (although, so, because) or a **voice** (active or passive). If you can tell which point a gap tests, you know which rule to use.

The other helpful thing about a passage is that it gives you clues. A time word such as "yesterday" or "since" tells you the tense. A word that was mentioned before tells you to use "the". Read the whole passage first, then fill the gaps.

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

The English Grammar section of the 2025 and 2026 test is reported as one passage with five blanks, worth 10 marks, in 10 minutes `[LIKELY]`. A 2025 preparation deck calls it "cloze test based", and coaches say it covers tenses, articles, prepositions and parts of speech `[REPORTED]`.

Nobody has published an actual cloze passage from that section, so the passages in this lesson are original. The closest official material is the set of fill-in-the-blank questions in the 2020 sample, which tested the same skills (see Lesson 12).

Ten marks for five blanks means two marks a blank, and ten minutes means two minutes a blank. That is a comfortable pace, so read the passage fully before you start.

## 3. The method

1. **Read the whole passage once**, ignoring the gaps. Get the story and the time (past, present or future).
2. **Go to blank 1. Name the job:** tense, article, preposition, connector or voice.
3. **Look for the clue.** Use the table below.
4. **Cross out the options that break a rule.**
5. **Read the sentence with your pick.** Does it also fit the sentence before and after?
6. **Move to the next blank.** Keep the same time and story in mind.

The clue table:

| The gap is | Look for | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| A tense | Words such as *yesterday*, *last year*, *since*, *by the time*, *by next June* | A named finished time: past simple. *Since* with something still true: present perfect. The earlier of two past events: past perfect. A future deadline: future perfect. |
| An article | Is this the first mention, or has it been mentioned before? Is the noun uncountable? | First mention of any one: *a/an*. A specific or known one: *the*. *Information* and *advice* take no *a*. |
| A preposition | The time or place word that follows | *In* with parts of the day, months and years. *On* with days and with a surface. *At* with clock times. *Since* with a starting point, *for* with a length. |
| A connector | Is the second idea a **result**, a **contrast** or a **reason**? | *So* for a result. *Although* and *but* for a contrast. *Because* for a reason. |
| Voice | Who does the action? | If the subject receives the action (books were copied), use *be* plus the past participle. |

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**Read the sentence after the gap.** Often it holds the clue, for example a time word. *It fails when the clue is two sentences away.* Scan the whole passage.

**Pick the tense once and keep it.** If the passage is in the past, most gaps will be past forms. *It fails when the passage shifts*, for example from a story to a general fact. Look for the shift word.

**First mention, then "the".** An unfamiliar noun gets *a*, and the same noun later gets *the*. *It fails for things everyone knows*, such as the sun or the internet, which take *the* straight away.

**Cross out two before you choose.** In most gaps, two options break a rule at once. *It fails when you cross out on feeling alone.* Name the rule.

## 5. Worked examples

Use this short passage with five gaps. These are judgement questions, each explained with the rule.

> Ravi bought **(1)** old bicycle last week. He had wanted one **(2)** he was in school. The shop owner gave him good **(3)**. Ravi cycles to work **(4)** Monday to Friday. By next month, he **(5)** more than two hundred kilometres.

**Example 1 (article).** The answer is **an**. *Old* begins with a vowel sound, and the bicycle has not been mentioned before, so *an* (not *the*).

**Example 2 (connector).** The answer is **since**. *He had wanted one since he was in school* gives a starting point in the past. *Because* would give a reason, which does not make sense here.

**Example 3 (uncountable noun).** The answer is **advice**. *Advice* has no plural form and takes no *a*. *Advices* and *an advice* are wrong.

**Example 4 (preposition).** The answer is **from**. *From Monday to Friday* marks the start and the end of a period.

**Example 5 (tense).** The answer is **will have cycled**. *By next month* names a future deadline, so the future perfect is used.

## 6. Traps

- **Choosing a tense without the clue word.** Find the time word first.
- **Using "the" for a first mention.** An unfamiliar noun gets *a* or *an*.
- **Confusing *since* and *for*.** A point in time takes *since*, a length takes *for*.
- **Picking a connector that reverses the sense.** Test it: does the second idea follow from the first, or go against it?
- **Mixing active and passive.** If the subject cannot do the action (books do not copy themselves), the verb must be passive.
- **Reading the gaps one by one without the passage.** The story gives the tense.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- Read the whole passage first. Name the job of each gap.
- Tenses: named finished time = past simple. *Since* + still true = present perfect. Earlier of two past events = past perfect. Future deadline = future perfect.
- Articles: first mention *a/an*, known thing *the*, uncountable no *a*.
- Prepositions: *in* parts of the day, *on* days and surfaces, *since* point, *for* length.
- Connectors: *so* result, *although* contrast, *because* reason.
- Passive = *be* + past participle.
