## 1. The idea

A data sufficiency question asks one thing: **is the information enough?** You do not have to find the answer. You only have to decide whether the facts you are given would let you find it, and whether there is exactly one possible answer.

There is a question, and two statements, called I and II. You judge:

- Is statement I alone enough?
- Is statement II alone enough?
- If neither is enough alone, are they enough together?

"Enough" means the question can only have **one** answer. If the facts leave two or more possible answers, they are not enough.

A good picture comes from the exam owners of a similar test, GMAC. They describe the two statements as two peepholes in a sealed box. Both look onto the same box. So the statements never contradict each other, and each shows only part of it.

## 2. What Infosys actually asks

In the official 2020 sample, five questions in the reasoning section were data sufficiency, and the page gave one answer scale `[CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]`:

- **1**: exactly one statement alone is enough;
- **2**: either statement alone is enough;
- **3**: both together are needed;
- **4**: even both together are not enough.

Do not mix this up with the GMAT's A to E options. Infosys uses numbers 1 to 4.

The five sample questions were about juice from orange trees (a cup is not a measure in litres, so the answer is 4), three consecutive integers in no fixed order (answer 3), coins (answer 1), the cost of copper in an alloy (answer 4) and whether 2000 was an average (answer 4). The PDF has no answer key, so these answers were worked out for this library; the juice one is a judgement call. One 2025 report asked for the smallest of four consecutive even numbers, with the sum given in statement I `[REPORTED]`.

Notice that, by these solutions, 4 appears three times out of five. Many of these questions are about spotting what is *missing*.

## 3. The method

1. **Read the question and decide what must be found.** Write it down: "I need x", or "I need the area".
2. **Test statement I on its own.** Ignore statement II completely. Does it leave exactly one possible answer?
3. **Test statement II on its own.** Ignore statement I.
4. **Count.** If both alone are enough, the mark is 2. If just one, the mark is 1.
5. **If neither alone is enough, put them together.** One answer only? The mark is 3. Still more than one? The mark is 4.

You are not solving the question. You are counting possible answers: none, one, or many. "One" is the only good result.

A table makes the marks easy to remember:

| Statement I alone | Statement II alone | Together | Mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| enough | enough | not needed | **2** |
| enough | not enough | not needed | **1** |
| not enough | enough | not needed | **1** |
| not enough | not enough | enough | **3** |
| not enough | not enough | not enough | **4** |

## 4. Shortcuts and when they fail

**Count the unknowns.** To find two unknowns (x and y) from equations you usually need two different equations. One equation with two unknowns usually leaves many answers. *It fails with squares, ranges and whole-number conditions*, which can pin down an answer from one equation. Check them one by one.

**Squares give two answers.** A statement such as x² = 16 gives x = 4 or x = −4, so it is not enough on its own, unless another fact says x is positive. *It fails if the question already tells you x is positive.* Read the question again.

**Inequalities rarely give one answer.** "x > 2" leaves many values. *It fails when combined with a statement that pins x to one value*, so use inequalities as filters.

**Check units and words.** A statement can sound helpful but be about the wrong thing, as in the sample's cup and litre question. *It fails when the units match*, so only worry when they do not.

## 5. Worked examples

All the answers were checked by trying every possible case by code, or by exact arithmetic.

**Example 1 (the mark is 3).** Rita has only 2-rupee and 5-rupee coins. How many 5-rupee coins does she have? I. She has 7 coins in all. II. The coins are worth Rs 26 in all.

Statement I alone: she could have 0 to 7 five-rupee coins, so not enough. Statement II alone: the number of five-rupee coins could be 0, 2 or 4, so not enough. Together: 7 coins worth Rs 26 gives only 4 five-rupee coins. Mark **3**.

**Example 2 (the mark is 2).** What is x? I. 3x + 4 = 19. II. x is positive and x² = 25.

Statement I: 3x = 15, so x = 5. Enough. Statement II: x² = 25 gives 5 or −5, and "positive" leaves 5. Enough. Mark **2**.

**Example 3 (the mark is 1).** What is x? I. x² = 16. II. 2x − 3 = 9.

Statement I gives 4 or −4, so it is not enough. Statement II gives x = 6, so it is enough. Mark **1**.

**Example 4 (the mark is 4).** What is x? I. x + y = 9. II. x > 2.

I leaves many pairs (1 and 8, 2 and 7, 3 and 6 and so on). II leaves many values. Together x could still be 3, 4, 5 and so on. Mark **4**.

**Example 5 (the mark is 3).** What is the area of a rectangle? I. The perimeter is 20. II. The length is twice the width.

Statement I alone: a width of 3 gives an area of 21, and a width of 4 gives 24. So not enough. Statement II alone: no size is fixed. Together: the length plus the width is 10 and the length is twice the width, so the width is 10 over 3. Then the area is one number, 200 over 9. Mark **3**.

## 6. Traps

- **Solving when you should be counting.** You only need to know if there is one answer, not what it is.
- **Using statement II while testing statement I.** Test each one alone first.
- **Saying "together" too early.** Together comes only when neither is enough alone.
- **Forgetting that squares give two answers.** x² = 16 means 4 or −4.
- **Trusting a sample-style answer without the units.** If a statement talks about cups and the question asks for litres, the information may not help.
- **Calling the mark 3 when one statement was already enough.** If statement II alone works, the mark is 1 or 2, never 3.

## 8. Recap in 60 seconds

- Count the possible answers. **One** is enough. More than one is not.
- Test I alone, then II alone, then together if you must.
- Marks: both alone = **2**; one alone = **1**; only together = **3**; not even together = **4**.
- One equation, two unknowns: usually not enough. A square gives two values unless something fixes the sign.
- You are judging, not solving. Do not waste time finding the answer.
