1. The idea

A fill-in-the-blank question gives you a sentence with a gap. You choose the word that fits. A cloze passage is the same idea in a paragraph, usually with five gaps.

Two things must be true for the right answer. It must be correct grammar, and it must make sense. Wrong options usually break one of those two. Some break both.

You do not need a huge vocabulary. You need a small set of grammar rules, and the habit of reading the words around the gap before you look at the options.

2. What Infosys actually asks

The official 2020 sample had four fill-in-the-blank items, in the verbal section under the heading "English Corrective Usage" [CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]. In plain terms they tested:

  • a noun that must match the phrase before it (a gap after "transaction costs and ...");
  • a plural subject that needs a plural verb phrase;
  • a gap where the meaning of the rest of the sentence points to one word (shoppers who walk in buy on impulse);
  • a fixed phrase, "a reversal of fortune".

The 2025 and 2026 test is reported to have a separate English Grammar section: one cloze passage with five blanks, worth 10 marks, in 10 minutes [LIKELY]. Coaches say the blanks test tenses, articles, prepositions and parts of speech [REPORTED]. That is about two minutes per blank.

So the work is the same in both: look for the grammar clue, then the meaning clue.

3. The method

  1. Read the whole sentence first. For a passage, read all of it once before you answer any blank.
  2. Name the job of the gap. Is it a noun, a verb, a preposition, an article or a joining word? The words just before and after the gap tell you.
  3. Check agreement. Is the subject singular or plural? Which time is the sentence in? A word that disagrees is wrong, however good it sounds.
  4. Check the meaning. Look for clue words such as "but", "although", "because" and "so". They tell you whether the idea continues or turns around.
  5. Drop the options that fail, then read your pick back inside the sentence.

The rules you will use most:

Question Rule
Time words since goes with a point in time (since 2019). for goes with a length (for five years). by is a deadline.
a or an? Go by the sound of the next word, not the spelling: an engineer, and an honorable peace, because the h is silent.
Singular or plural verb? each, either, neither, everyone, nobody take a singular verb. Ignore a phrase between the subject and the verb. With nor, agree with the nearer subject.
Words with no plural information, advice, knowledge and help have no plural form and take a singular verb.
Which tense? A time that is named and finished (yesterday, in 2018) needs the past simple. A state that began earlier and is still true (since 2019) needs the present perfect. The earlier of two past events takes the past perfect. A deadline in the future takes the future perfect.

4. Shortcuts and when they fail

Look at the neighbours. If the word after the gap is a noun, the gap probably needs an article or an adjective. If the word before is a helper such as has, the gap needs a past participle. It fails when a short phrase is stuffed between them, so read a little wider.

Use the clue word. "Although" and "but" mean a turn. "Because" and "so" mean a result. "And" means the idea carries on. Pick the option that does the same job. It fails when two options both do that job. Then fall back on grammar.

Read it back. Say the whole sentence in your head with your answer in the gap. Your ear often catches a wrong ending. It fails for look-alike words such as affect and effect, where both sound fine.

Cross out the worst two first. Most questions have two options that clearly break a rule. Two choices are easier than four. It fails when you cross out on a hunch. Cross out only for a reason you can name.

5. Worked examples

These are judgement questions, so each answer comes with its reason.

Example 1 (time word). "She has been working here ___ 2019." The options are since, for, during, by.

2019 is a point in time. That needs since. Compare "for five years", which names a length.

Example 2 (singular subject). "Each of the students ___ a laptop." The options are has, have, are having, were.

Each is singular. The words "of the students" are only a phrase in between. The verb is has.

Example 3 (article and uncountable noun). "My brother is ___ engineer." The answer is an, because the word engineer starts with a vowel sound. And in "The ___ you gave me was useful", the word is information. It has no plural, and the verb was is singular.

Example 4 (clue word). "She was tired ___ she had slept for ten hours." The options are although, because, so, unless.

Sleeping for ten hours should make you less tired. So the second part goes against the first. Only although shows that contrast.

Example 5 (two past events). "By the time the audience arrived, the speaker ___ the final slide." The options are had finished, has finished, finishes, will finish.

Two things happened in the past. The speaker finishing came first. The audience arriving came second. The earlier event takes the past perfect: had finished.

6. Traps

  • Choosing the word that sounds fancy. The right word is the one that fits the grammar and the meaning, not the longest one.
  • Matching the wrong noun. In "The report, along with the documents, ___ sent", the subject is report. Do not match the verb to documents.
  • Since and for. Many people mix them. A date or a time (2019, Monday) goes with since. A length (ten years) goes with for.
  • Present perfect with a finished time. "I have seen him yesterday" is wrong. When the time is named and finished, use the past simple.
  • Look-alike words. Affect (to influence) and effect (a result, or to bring about) are the classic pair. Check what job the gap has.

8. Recap in 60 seconds

  • Two tests for every answer: correct grammar and sensible meaning.
  • Read the whole sentence. Name the job of the gap. Check agreement. Check the clue word. Read it back.
  • since = point in time, for = length, by = deadline.
  • an before a vowel sound. information and advice have no plural.
  • Finished time named: past simple. Still true now: present perfect. Earlier of two past events: past perfect.
  • The subject is the main noun, not the noun closest to the verb.
