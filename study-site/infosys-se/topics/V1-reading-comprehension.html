V1 — Reading comprehension

Tier 1 · Verbal Ability · 6.8% of marks · about 1.2 h · confidence Medium

Adapted from L&T/lt-prep-library/topics/V1-reading-comprehension.md; verified in that library's QA. §1 and §10 are regenerated for Infosys from data/weightage.json; §2 gains an Infosys evidence table; §3–§9 are the source text unchanged, so "observed" in the worked examples refers to the source paper.

1. Why this topic matters here

Expect about 5.1 questions and 5.1 marks on this topic (about 6.8% of the 75 scored marks). It sits in Verbal Ability: 20 questions, 20 marks, 20 minutes. Tier 1, 1.2 hours to reach reliable recall, confidence Medium.

5 questions on this topic are in the observed Infosys pool (O41, O42, O43, O44, O45), from 1 source. They are listed in §2 and solved in ../question-banks/QB-99-OBSERVED-POOL.md.

Why the tier: expected marks per study hour, weighted by confidence, is 3.39 against a paper average of 1.84 (../data/analysis-tables.md §6). Every section has its own cut-off, so a zero here costs the whole section's chance, not only these marks. There is no calculator. Negative marking: none reported.

Passage topics are almost always one of: business and economics, technology, environment, education, or a social-science extract. Difficulty lives in the answer options, not the passage.

3. Concept notes

3.1 What the question is actually testing

Not reading speed. It tests whether you can find a line, decide whether a claim is stated or only implied, and eliminate three options that are almost right. Every wrong option is built by taking something from the passage and changing one thing — a quantity, a subject, a strength word, or a direction. Find the change and you have the answer.

3.2 Read the question first

Four-step routine, every time:

  1. Read the question stem only (not the options).
  2. Find the keyword in the stem — usually a proper noun, a number, or a distinctive noun phrase.
  3. Scan the passage for that keyword or its synonym. Answer is within two lines of it.
  4. Read the options and eliminate.

Reading the whole passage first is not wrong, it is just slower. On a 300-word passage with four questions you will re-read the passage anyway; reading it first means reading it twice.

3.3 Stated vs inferred

The boundary is the single highest-value rule here.

  • Stated: the passage says it. The option is a close paraphrase. Example: passage says "cash-handling costs fell by ₹1.2 crore" → option says "cash handling became cheaper."
  • Inferred: the passage does not say it, but two facts together force it. Example: passage says most new card users previously bought paper tickets → option says "the cards did not necessarily bring in new riders."

If an option needs one extra assumption beyond the passage text, it is wrong, even if it is true in real life. Real-world truth is not evidence.

3.4 Strength words decide close calls

When two options look right, the difference is usually degree.

  • Passage says "most" → option saying "all" is wrong.
  • Passage says "can" → option saying "does" is wrong.
  • Passage says "in some studies" → option saying "research shows" is wrong.

Words that kill an option: all, never, always, only, proves, entirely, must, impossible, no one. Words that usually survive: some, many, often, can, may, tends to, suggests, likely.

3.5 The four distractor types

Every wrong option is one of these. Name the type and you eliminate it in two seconds.

Type What it does Example
True but not in passage Sounds like common sense, isn't supported Passage on fare cards → option "senior citizens travel less than others"
Half right Correct wording, wrong quantity or subject Passage says 8% fall in complex tasks → option says "8% fall in total tasks"
Reversal Swaps cause and effect, or gain and loss Passage says standards raise switching costs → option says standards reduce them
Extreme Adds a strength or scope word Passage says "this is not an argument against standards" → option "the author opposes standards"

3.6 Tone and attitude vocabulary

Attitude questions are answered from the author's adjectives and connectives, not from the topic.

Tone word Means Signal in the text
Critical but constructive Points out a flaw, offers a fix "This is not an argument against X. It is an argument for..."
Sceptical Doubts a widely held claim "has been widely quoted without that caveat"
Cautious / measured Hedges every claim "may", "suggests", "the authors caution"
Enthusiastic Unqualified approval "remarkable", "a decisive advance"
Neutral / descriptive Reports, does not judge Only verbs and numbers, no evaluative adjectives
Ironic Says the opposite of what it means Praise that contradicts the evidence given

If the author offers both a criticism and a remedy, the answer is critical but constructive. If the author only criticises, it is critical or dismissive.

3.7 Vocabulary in context

Never use the first dictionary meaning. Use the sentence's own signals.

  • Contrast signal (but, whereas, although): the word means the opposite of the other half. Example: "The report was terse, where its predecessor ran to ninety pages" → terse = brief.
  • Cause signal (because, since): the meaning is explained after the signal.
  • Definition signal (dash, colon, that is): the meaning is stated outright.

3.8 Transition words carry the logic

Scanning for these four sets is faster than reading the passage.

Set Words What follows is
Contrast but, however, yet, although, whereas, despite The author's real point — usually the answer
Cause because, since, therefore, thus, hence A conclusion, often the main idea
Addition moreover, furthermore, in addition More of the same; rarely the answer
Concession admittedly, of course, it is true that A point the author will then knock down

The sentence after a contrast word is where main-idea and inference answers live.

3.9 Time budget

At 60 seconds per question:

Task Time
Read question stem + find keyword 10 s
Scan to answer line 20 s
Eliminate four options 20 s
Mark and move 10 s

If you are past 90 seconds, pick the option that best matches the passage's own wording and move. With no negative marking, never leave a blank.

4. Must-memorise block

Four-step routine: read stem → find keyword → scan to line → eliminate.

Stated vs inferred: if it needs one assumption, it is wrong.

Strength killers: all, never, always, only, proves, entirely, must, impossible, no one. Strength survivors: some, many, often, can, may, tends to, suggests, likely.

Four distractors: true-but-unsupported, half-right, reversal, extreme.

Contrast words → answer lives here: but, however, yet, although, whereas, despite, nonetheless.

Concession pattern: "Admittedly... but..." → the "but" half is the author's view.

Tone shortcuts: criticism + remedy = critical but constructive. Only criticism = dismissive. Only numbers = neutral.

Vocabulary in context: use the contrast signal, not the dictionary.

EXCEPT questions: three options are supported; find the one that is not. Answer the leftover.

Never leave a blank. No negative marking.

5. Worked examples

Example 1 — Vocabulary in context

Passage: "The committee's second report was terse, running to four pages where its first had run to ninety." Q: The word "terse" most nearly means — (a) rude (b) brief (c) evasive (d) hurried.

Fast method. Find the contrast signal where. The sentence sets four pages against ninety. The contrast is length, so terse means short → (b) brief. Why the slow method loses. Looking up "terse" gives "brief and to the point, sometimes curtly so." Both (a) and (b) are then live, and you burn 40 seconds deciding. The passage settles it in five.

Example 2 — Stated detail

Passage: "The plant was commissioned in 2016 and reached full capacity in 2019, two years behind schedule." Q: When did the plant reach full capacity? (a) 2016 (b) 2017 (c) 2019 (d) 2021.

Fast method. Keyword is "full capacity." Scan, hit it, read the date next to it → (c) 2019. The "two years behind schedule" phrase is noise. Option (b) 2017 is the trap for anyone who subtracts instead of reading. Why the slow method loses. Reading for meaning invites you to work out the planned date. The question asked for the actual date.

Example 3 — Inference

Passage: "During the 2009 downturn the firm cut advertising by 40%. Its largest competitor held spend flat. By 2011 the competitor's share had risen four points while the firm's had fallen three." Q: The passage implies that —

Fast method. Two facts: one firm cut, the other did not; one lost share, the other gained. The only link the passage supplies is the advertising decision → "cutting advertising during a downturn can cost market share." Anything stronger, such as "advertising always pays for itself," is an extreme option and dies on the "always." Why the slow method loses. Trying to reason from real marketing theory brings in outside knowledge. The question only wants what these two sentences force.

Example 4 — EXCEPT

Passage: "To claim the subsidy a farmer had to register online, submit land records, and hold a bank account in their own name." Q: All of the following were required EXCEPT — (a) online registration (b) land records (c) a bank account in the farmer's name (d) smartphone ownership.

Fast method. Three options map onto the three listed requirements. The fourth does not → (d). Note that online registration implies a device, but the passage never requires a smartphone. Implication is not requirement. Why the slow method loses. Reading for general sense makes (d) feel implied, and the EXCEPT question then has two answers.

Example 5 — Tone

Passage: "The scheme's targets were met. Whether they were the right targets is another matter; the ministry has published no data on outcomes, and until it does, the celebration is premature." Q: The author's tone is best described as — (a) enthusiastic (b) neutral (c) critical (d) hostile.

Fast method. The author concedes a fact ("targets were met"), then attacks the basis for celebrating, without calling the scheme a failure. Concession plus a pointed caveat → (c) critical. Hostile would require a claim that the scheme failed. Why the slow method loses. Judging tone from the topic (a government scheme) rather than from the author's own phrasing.

6. Traps and gotchas

Trap Why it catches people How to avoid it
Reading the whole passage first Burns 45-60 seconds you cannot recover Read the stem, then scan for the keyword
Choosing the "true in real life" option World knowledge feels like evidence Ask: which line of the passage says this?
Missing a strength word "all" vs "most" looks identical under time pressure Circle all, only, never, always, proves in every option
Confusing stated with inferred Inference feels obvious once you have read it If you had to supply one idea, it is not stated
EXCEPT questions Two options look supported Find the three that are explicitly listed; take the leftover
Topic-word matching An option loaded with passage vocabulary feels right Correct options usually paraphrase; wrong ones copy
Vocabulary "common sense" meaning The familiar sense is often a trap Use the contrast or definition signal in the sentence
Tone judged from topic Any passage about a problem sounds critical Judge only from the author's adjectives and concessions

7. Practice questions

Passage 1 (Q1-Q4) Metro Transit Authority introduced contactless fare cards in 2019. Ridership rose 6% in the first year. The authority's own audit, however, notes that most of that rise came from riders shifting away from paper tickets rather than from new riders. Cash-handling costs fell by ₹1.2 crore a year. The authority now plans to withdraw paper tickets entirely by 2024, a move senior citizens' groups oppose, arguing that many of their members pay in cash and would rather keep doing so.

1. The audit's finding suggests that the 6% ridership rise a) proves the cards attracted new commuters b) was smaller than the authority claimed c) was largely a redistribution of existing riders d) came entirely from senior citizens

2. The authority's plan for 2024 is best described as a) a subsidy for senior citizens b) a response to falling revenue c) a phased expansion to other cities d) a complete withdrawal of paper tickets

3. The senior citizens' groups' position implies that they a) oppose all contactless payment on principle b) believe the cards are overpriced c) value the option of paying in cash d) want the ridership figures audited

4. Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the claim that the fare cards did not expand the rider base? a) Paper tickets cost the same as card rides b) Cash-handling savings exceeded projections c) Surveys show 4% of card users had never used the bus before d) Senior citizens use the bus less often than other riders

Passage 2 (Q5-Q8) Studies of remote work usually measure output as tasks completed. A two-year study of 900 software engineers found that remote workers completed 13% more tasks than office-based peers, but that their share of high-complexity tasks — architectural design, cross-team debugging — fell by 8%. Managers rated remote workers lower on collaboration. The authors caution that task counts reward volume rather than judgement, and that the 13% figure has been quoted widely without that caveat.

5. The passage's main point is that a) remote work reduces productivity b) a widely cited productivity gain rests on a measure its authors qualify c) software engineers prefer working from home d) managers are biased against remote workers

6. The 8% fall refers to a) overall task completion b) collaboration ratings c) the number of engineers studied d) the share of high-complexity tasks

7. The phrase "without that caveat" suggests that the 13% figure a) has been used to imply remote work is better across the board b) was fabricated c) applies only to architects d) was measured over two years

8. The authors would most likely agree that a) task counts are the best available productivity measure b) productivity measures should match the kind of work being judged c) collaboration ratings should replace output measures d) remote work should be discontinued for engineers

Passage 3 (Q9-Q12) Standards are usually described as a floor below which quality does not fall. In practice they also act as a ceiling. Once a specification is fixed and certified, the cost of departing from it rises: suppliers tool up for it, auditors test against it, buyers compare on it. The result is that a standard can lock in the best-known method of the moment and make the next method expensive to adopt. This is not an argument against standards. It is an argument for writing them so that they specify outcomes rather than methods, and for reviewing them on a fixed cycle rather than on demand.

9. The author's central claim is that standards a) lower quality over time b) should be abolished in fast-moving industries c) are written mainly to help suppliers d) can constrain innovation as well as guarantee minimum quality

10. The phrase "act as a ceiling" is supported by all of the following EXCEPT a) standards guarantee minimum quality b) auditors test against it c) suppliers tool up for the specification d) buyers compare on it

11. The author recommends a) fewer standards overall b) outcome-based specifications and fixed review cycles c) removing auditors from the certification process d) letting buyers set the specifications

12. The author's attitude is best described as a) critical but constructive b) wholesale rejection c) neutral indifference d) unqualified enthusiasm

ANSWER KEY

  1. c — the audit says most of the rise came from riders shifting off paper tickets, i.e. redistribution, not expansion.
  2. d — the passage says the authority plans to withdraw paper tickets entirely by 2024.
  3. c — they argue members pay in cash "and would rather keep doing so," which is about retaining the cash option, not about pricing.
  4. c — evidence of genuinely new riders directly undercuts the claim that no new riders appeared; the other options are irrelevant to that claim.
  5. b — the passage's core is that the 13% gain rests on task counts, a measure the authors themselves qualify.
  6. d — the 8% fall is in the share of high-complexity tasks, named in the same clause.
  7. a — "without that caveat" means the figure is quoted as if it settled the whole question of remote-work productivity.
  8. b — the caution that task counts reward volume rather than judgement supports matching the measure to the work.
  9. d — the author says a standard can lock in the current method while also setting a quality floor.
  10. a — the floor claim is the first sentence, not evidence for the ceiling claim; the other three are listed as costs of departing.
  11. b — the last sentence explicitly recommends outcomes over methods and fixed-cycle review.
  12. a — the author identifies a flaw and then proposes a remedy, which is criticism plus construction.

9. Self-check

  1. Can you state the four-step routine without looking it up?
  2. Can you name the four distractor types and give an example of each?
  3. Do you know the strength words that kill an option, and the ones that usually survive?
  4. Can you tell the difference between a stated-detail option and an inference option in under five seconds?
  5. Can you handle an EXCEPT question by finding three supported options rather than hunting for the wrong one?
  6. Can you finish one passage plus its questions in 60 seconds per question without re-reading the passage?

If you answered no to any of 1, 3, or 5, revise section 4 again. Everything else improves with ten timed passages.
