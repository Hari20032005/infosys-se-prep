Lesson 16. Reading comprehension

Tier 1 · Verbal Ability · 6.8% of marks · about 1.2 h · confidence Medium

Topic V1 · about 5.1 of the 75 marks · plan about 1.2 hours.

1. The idea

In a reading comprehension question, you read a short passage and answer questions about it. The answer is always in the passage, or follows directly from it. You are not tested on what you know about the world.

Treat the passage as the only source of truth. If an option sounds right but the passage does not say it, it is wrong. If an option sounds odd but the passage says it, it is right.

Most questions fall into five kinds:

  • A fact. The passage states it. Find the sentence.
  • The main idea. What is the whole passage about?
  • An inference. Something that must be true because of what the passage says.
  • A word in context. What does a word mean in this sentence?
  • The writer's attitude. Is the writer for, against, or neutral?

2. What Infosys actually asks

The official 2020 sample had one passage with five questions, about the plays of a famous writer [CONFIRMED — official sample 2020]. Four of the five were about facts stated in the passage: what was noted about the techniques, what the plays are about, which statement matches the passage, and which play is named for a certain joke. The fifth asked why the plays are treasured, and the answer was in the closing sentence.

So the sample was mostly about finding the sentence that says it. The passage was longer than the ones here, but the skills are the same.

The Verbal section is 20 questions in 20 minutes. A passage with five questions is a quarter of the section, and each question gets about a minute.

3. The method

  1. Read the passage once, fast. Note the first sentence, the last sentence, and what each paragraph is about.
  2. Read the question and all four options before you go back to the passage.
  3. Find the lines that answer it. Put a finger on them.
  4. Match the options against those lines. Cross out any option that says something the lines do not say, or says more than they do.
  5. For an inference, ask: must this be true? If it only might be true, it is not the answer.
  6. For a word in context, put each option in the sentence. The one that keeps the meaning is the answer.

Traps to scan for in the options:

Trap Example
Too strong "always", "never", "all", "only" when the passage says "many", "often"
Not in the passage A true fact about the world that the passage does not mention
Half right The first half matches, the second half does not
Wrong person or number A number or a name taken from a different sentence

4. Shortcuts and when they fail

The first and last sentences. They often give the main idea and the writer's view. They fail when the passage opens with a story or a quotation. Read the middle too.

Look for the contrast word. "However", "but" and "yet" mark a turn. The writer's view is usually after the turn. It fails when the passage has several turns. Follow the last one.

Cross out the extremes first. Options with "always" or "never" are usually wrong. They fail when the passage itself uses a strong word.

Skim, then search. Do not memorise the passage. Know where things are. It fails for inference questions, which need the whole passage in mind.

5. Worked examples

Use this short passage.

The old railway station at Mandya closed in 2012, and for years its hall stood empty. Last spring a group of local artists asked the town to let them use it. They painted the walls, hung lamps from the roof beams, and began to hold a market there every Sunday. Within months, the market had sixty stalls. Not everyone is pleased: some shopkeepers on the main road say they have lost customers. The artists reply that the market brings more visitors to the town, and that the shopkeepers will gain in the end.

These are judgement questions, each explained with the lines it rests on.

Example 1 (a fact). When did the station close? 2012. The first sentence says so.

Example 2 (an inference). What can be inferred about the market? It attracted many sellers quickly. The market had sixty stalls "within months". The passage never says how many people visit, so "it is crowded every day" would be too strong.

Example 3 (the main idea). Which sentence is the best summary? A disused station became a market, which pleases some people and worries others. It covers the empty hall, the artists, and the shopkeepers' complaint. A summary that mentions only the artists leaves out half of the passage.

Example 4 (a word in context). In "its hall stood empty", what does "stood" mean? Remained. Try "was lifted up" and "was upright": neither fits a hall that was unused for years. "Remained empty" fits.

Example 5 (the writer's attitude). What is the writer's tone? Balanced. The writer reports what the artists did, what the shopkeepers said, and what the artists replied. There is no word that favours one side.

6. Traps

  • Choosing what you know. A fact about railways that the passage does not mention is not an answer.
  • Choosing the first option that sounds right. Read all four.
  • Mixing up speakers. The shopkeepers complain, and the artists reply. Check who said what.
  • Missing the "NOT" in a question. Underline it before you read the options.
  • Going back too late. If you are unsure, return to the lines at once, not after you have talked yourself into an answer.

7. Practice questions

Attempt each level before opening the key. Level 3 is harder than anything in the 2020 official sample, on purpose: if you can do Level 3, the real paper will feel easy.

Level 1: warm-up

Facts stated in a short, plain passage.

Passage 1

A small town in Karnataka started a mobile library three years ago. A van carries about two thousand books and visits twelve villages each week. Children borrow most of the books, but many adults now come to the van too, mainly for books on farming and health. The library has one rule: every borrower must return a book before taking a new one. This keeps the stock moving, and almost no books are lost. The town council pays for the van and the driver. A local school lends two teachers on Saturdays to help children choose books. Last year the library lent more than forty thousand books. The organisers now want a second van, but they have not found the money for it yet.

1. [Fact] How many villages does the van visit each week?
a) Two
b) Twelve
c) Three
d) Forty

2. [Fact] Who borrows most of the books?
a) Teachers
b) Children
c) Farmers
d) Council staff

3. [Fact] What is the library's one rule?
a) No book may be borrowed twice
b) Each book may be kept for one week only
c) Children may borrow only on Saturdays
d) A borrower must return a book before taking a new one

4. [Fact] Who pays for the van and the driver?
a) The borrowers
b) The organisers
c) A local school
d) The town council

5. [Fact] What do the organisers want now?
a) A second van, for which they have not found the money
b) A new town council
c) More teachers on Saturdays
d) A rule change about lost books

Level 2: exam level

A main idea, a stated reason, a "not mentioned" question, a word in context and a detail.

Passage 2

When the firm let its staff work from home two days a week, the managers expected a drop in output. Instead, the monthly reports showed a rise of eight percent in finished projects, and the number of sick days fell. Employees said they saved about ninety minutes a day that they would have spent travelling. However, new staff found it harder to learn the firm's habits, because much of that learning happens through casual talk in the office. To fix this, the firm now asks new staff to come in every day for their first three months. Managers also meet teams weekly, so that problems are raised early instead of being left to grow. The firm has not yet decided whether to allow three days at home; it wants to wait for another six months of results before choosing.

6. [Main idea] Which statement best gives the main idea of the passage?
a) The firm has decided to let staff work from home three days a week.
b) Working from home made output fall, so the firm ended it.
c) Working from home two days a week improved results, and the firm added steps to help new staff.
d) New staff are the only people who gain from working at home.

7. [Reason] Why must new staff come in every day for their first three months?
a) Much of the learning about the firm's habits happens through casual talk in the office.
b) They were less productive at home than other staff.
c) They travel less than other staff.
d) Managers do not trust new staff.

8. [Not mentioned] Which of these is NOT mentioned as a result of working from home?
a) Fewer sick days
b) Higher output
c) Lower office rent
d) Time saved on travel

9. [Word in context] In the passage, "raised" in "problems are raised early" most nearly means
a) grown like crops
b) made larger
c) brought up for discussion
d) lifted physically

10. [Detail] What does the firm plan to do about allowing three days at home?
a) Refuse it for new staff only
b) Wait for six more months of results before choosing
c) Allow it from next month
d) Ask staff to vote now

Level 3: beyond the exam

A passage that gives an argument, with attitude, assumption, weakening and inference questions.

Passage 3

Many teachers now argue that frequent short quizzes help students more than one long exam at the end of the year. A short quiz forces a student to recall facts soon after learning them, and studies of memory suggest that recalling a fact strengthens it far more than reading it again. Opponents reply that quizzes make students anxious and turn learning into a race for marks. This worry deserves attention, but it rests on a weak assumption: that quizzes must carry heavy marks. If a quiz counts for little, or nothing, the anxiety largely disappears while the benefit of recall remains. The real question, then, is not whether to use quizzes, but how to weigh them. A school that treats each quiz as a way to check understanding, rather than a way to rank students, is likely to keep the benefit and lose most of the stress.

11. [Main idea] Which statement best gives the author's main point?
a) Frequent quizzes can help learning without much stress if they carry few marks.
b) Quizzes cannot be used without making students anxious.
c) Long exams at the end of the year are better than quizzes.
d) Students should never be ranked.

12. [Attitude] How does the author treat the opponents' worry about anxiety?
a) As a worry with no basis at all
b) As proof that quizzes should be dropped
c) As a worry the author has not understood
d) As a worry worth attention, but one that can be answered

13. [Detail] Which assumption does the author call weak?
a) That recalling a fact strengthens it
b) That exams come at the end of the year
c) That quizzes must carry heavy marks
d) That teachers support quizzes

14. [Weaken] Which finding would most weaken the author's argument?
a) Students remember facts better after a quiz
b) Students become anxious even about quizzes that carry no marks
c) Quizzes take only ten minutes
d) Many schools already hold weekly quizzes

15. [Inference] With which statement would the author most likely agree?
a) A school should use quizzes mainly to check understanding.
b) A school should replace all lessons with quizzes.
c) A school should rank students after every quiz.
d) A school should avoid quizzes altogether.

ANSWER KEY

  1. b — Judgement: The passage says the van "visits twelve villages each week". The other numbers belong to other facts (two thousand books, forty thousand loans, three years).
  2. b — Judgement: "Children borrow most of the books." Adults come too, mainly for farming and health books.
  3. d — Judgement: The passage states the rule directly: return a book before taking a new one.
  4. d — Judgement: "The town council pays for the van and the driver." The school only lends two teachers.
  5. a — Judgement: The last sentence says they want a second van but have not found the money.
  6. c — Judgement: The passage reports better output, then a problem for new staff and the firm's fix. It has not decided on three days, and output did not fall.
  7. a — Judgement: The passage gives this reason directly: casual talk in the office is where the habits are learned.
  8. c — Judgement: Output (up eight percent), sick days (fewer) and travel time (about ninety minutes a day) are all stated. Rent is never mentioned.
  9. c — Judgement: Substitute each option: managers meet teams so that problems are brought up early instead of being left to grow. The other meanings do not fit.
  10. b — Judgement: The last sentence says it will wait for another six months of results.
  11. a — Judgement: The author backs quizzes, answers the anxiety worry by lowering the stakes, and says the question is how to weigh them.
  12. d — Judgement: "This worry deserves attention, but it rests on a weak assumption": taken seriously, then answered.
  13. c — Judgement: The sentence after "a weak assumption" names it: "that quizzes must carry heavy marks".
  14. b — Judgement: The author says anxiety "largely disappears" when quizzes carry little or no weight. Finding anxiety even then attacks that claim. The other options do not.
  15. a — Judgement: The last sentence says a school that uses quizzes to check understanding, not to rank, keeps the benefit and loses most of the stress.

8. Recap in 60 seconds

  • The passage is the only source of truth.
  • Read once for the gist, read the question, find the lines, match the options.
  • Inference means must be true, not might be true.
  • Word in context: put each option into the sentence.
  • Watch for "always", "never", "all" and "only", and for the "NOT" in a question.
